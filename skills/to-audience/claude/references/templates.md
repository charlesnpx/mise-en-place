# Format skeletons

Structural skeletons only. Keep the skeleton, vary the surface wording — do not copy headings verbatim into every artifact. Visual styling, chart design, and brand voice are out of scope (see Lane separation in SKILL.md).

## Memo / report

> **[Title states the recommendation, not the topic]**
>
> *(If audience or purpose was inferred, state it here: "Written for: … deciding whether to …")*
>
> **Recommendation** — one sentence: the decision you want, scoped, with the smallest viable ask.
>
> **Situation** — 2–4 sentences of facts the reader already accepts, rebuilt from zero. Introduce every system, team, project, and acronym as if the reader has never seen this work.
>
> **Complication** — what the status quo now costs, in the audience's value frame (money, risk, speed, compliance, outcomes).
>
> **Why believe this** — 2–4 grouped reasons (three by default; never pad weak reasons). Each is a claim→evidence→implication block: the assertion, the reader-legible proof (number, table, quote, excerpt), and what it means for this decision.
>
> **Tradeoffs and risks** — concede the real ones; state mitigations and the stop condition.
>
> **The strongest objection** — stated in a form its proponents would recognize, then answered: "Some will argue X; that is not decisive because Y; the evidence is Z." (More prompts: `objection-bank.md`.)
>
> **Ask** — concrete, small, reversible where possible: resources, timeline, success metrics, review date.

## Deck (8-slide default)

Test before finalizing: reading only the slide titles, top to bottom, must reconstruct the full argument.

| Slide | Title pattern (always a full-sentence conclusion) | Body (one visual proof object; no bullet walls) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *[Status quo] is now the bottleneck to [what this audience cares about].* | The one chart or metric that shows it |
| 2 | *The cause is [root cause], not [the sympathetic misdiagnosis].* | Diagram or table of the current state |
| 3 | *[Proposal] fixes the common case without breaking the exceptions.* | Before/after comparison |
| 4 | *The first step targets [narrow, high-value scope].* | Included / excluded scope table |
| 5 | *The economics work even at partial adoption.* | Cost/benefit figures |
| 6 | *The strongest objection — [objection] — is real and answered.* | Objection / response / evidence table |
| 7 | *The ask is a reversible [pilot/trial], not a commitment.* | Scope, owners, timeline, success and stop criteria |
| 8 | *Approve [the ask]; results reviewed [date].* | The decision options |

Compress or expand slide count as needed, but never drop: the answer up front (1–3), the objection slide (6), the decision slide (8).

## Website / static page

1. **Hero** — headline states the answer/value proposition; subhead carries the single most compelling piece of evidence; primary CTA is the smallest concrete ask.
2. **Problem** — the complication and its cost, with one legible proof.
3. **Body sections** — one claim/evidence/implication block per section; every section heading is a full-sentence assertion.
4. **Proof** — evidence the reader can inspect: numbers, before/after, near-peer examples (only if genuinely comparable).
5. **FAQ** — visible (accordion is fine); the hardest objections answered refutationally, not deflected.
6. **CTA** — restated ask: specific, low-friction, reversible; say what happens immediately after the reader acts.

## Conversation prep

You produce a prep document for the user to carry into the room; you are never in the room. Structure:

- **Goal** — the decision or next step the conversation should reach, and the fallback outcome that still counts as progress.
- **Their position** — current belief, what they're accountable for, their value frame.
- **Questions to ask first** — open questions that surface their reasoning before any pitch (e.g. "What would have to be true for this to be worth doing?", "What worries you most about it?", "What evidence would change your mind?").
- **Anticipated concerns** — each with (a) a reflection that shows the concern was heard, and (b) the response with its evidence.
- **Evidence to bring** — the specific artifacts, numbers, or excerpts to have on hand, reader-legible.
- **The small next step to propose** — concrete, reversible, with a review point.

## Conditional elements (use only if they clarify stakes)

- **Compact narrative** — one only, and short: a single person/workflow, before and after. Pattern: "Today, [person] spends [time] on [pain]. Afterwards, [same person] [better outcome] by default." Never let the story carry the argument.
- **Near-peer social proof** — only from contexts of similar scale and risk profile; a distant or glamorous example reads as decoration, not evidence.
